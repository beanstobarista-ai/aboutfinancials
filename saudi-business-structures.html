Saudi business structures: choosing the right legal form

The legal form you choose decides how exposed you are personally, how easily you can bring in partners or investors, how much governance you carry and what it costs to change course later. The 2022 Companies Law gave Saudi founders genuinely modern options. This guide explains how experienced advisers choose between them.

Information as of 10 October 2026 · Checked against official Saudi sources · About 13 min read

At a glance

  • Most owner-managed and foreign-owned businesses choose a limited liability company: one owner or several, liability limited to capital, managed by one or more managers.
  • Startups planning to raise money increasingly choose a simplified joint-stock company: no statutory minimum capital, governance you design yourself, and share restrictions, compulsory transfers and arbitration that investors expect.
  • A Saudi sole trader can start as an establishment, but the business’s debts stay with the owner personally.
  • Joint-stock companies need issued capital of at least SAR 500,000. Partnerships expose general partners to unlimited personal liability.
  • Foreign ownership adds MISA registration and, for some activities, minimum capital or Saudi ownership conditions.

The menu: what Saudi law offers

The Companies Law recognises five company forms: the general partnership, the limited partnership, the joint-stock company, the simplified joint-stock company and the limited liability company. Every company formed under it is Saudi, with its headquarters in the Kingdom, even when its owners are foreign. Alongside those five, a Saudi individual can trade as an establishment registered in their own name, and a foreign company can register a branch.

The law also lets you overlay additional arrangements on whichever form you choose. Partners can sign shareholder agreements that govern their relationship and how heirs join, and family businesses can adopt a family charter covering ownership, governance, employment of family members and dispute resolution. Both are binding and can be built into the articles. A holding company is not a separate form either: it is an LLC, joint-stock or simplified joint-stock company that owns subsidiaries.

Capital can still matter even when the Companies Law sets no minimum. For foreign-owned businesses, MISA applies its own minimum capital and Saudi ownership conditions to certain activities, most notably trading. We cover those below.

The five questions that decide it

Rather than starting from the forms, start from your situation. Five questions settle the choice for nearly every founder.

  1. How much personal exposure can you accept? If the business will sign leases, take supplier credit or carry liability risk, you want a company, not an establishment or a general partnership.
  2. Who owns it now, and who will own it in three years? One owner, a few partners, a family, or a cap table of investors and employees each point to a different form.
  3. Will you raise outside capital? Investors look for share classes, transfer restrictions, drag-along and tag-along rights and predictable exits. The simplified joint-stock company was built for this, though LLCs now offer some of it too.
  4. How much governance do you want to carry? A joint-stock company has board and general assembly formalities. LLCs and simplified joint-stock companies let you keep things lean.
  5. Is any owner foreign? Foreign ownership adds MISA registration, an auditor requirement and possibly activity-specific capital or ownership conditions.

The establishment: fast, simple and personal

An establishment is the simplest way for a Saudi individual to trade. It is registered in the Commercial Register in the owner’s name, so there are no articles, no shareholders and no company governance. For a freelancer, a small shop or a first test of an idea, that simplicity is attractive.

The trade-off is personal liability. The establishment’s obligations are the owner’s obligations. If you later move the business into a company, the Companies Law lets you transfer the establishment’s assets into it, but it is explicit that you are not released from debts incurred before the company was formed unless your creditors agree. If the business is going to take on real commitments, it is usually cleaner to start as a single-owner LLC.

The limited liability company: the default choice

The LLC is the workhorse of the Saudi market, and for good reason. It can be formed by one person or several, individuals or companies. Its assets and liabilities are separate from its owners’, who are liable only in proportion to their share of the capital. The partners set the capital in the articles of incorporation, divided into equal interests that cannot be traded like shares, and they acknowledge in the articles that their contributions are fully paid.

Management is light. One or more managers run the company, appointed from among the partners or from outside, either in the articles or by separate contract. If there are several managers, the partners can form a board of managers, but there is no requirement for a board of directors. A single-owner LLC goes further: the owner holds all the powers of the manager and the general assembly, records decisions in writing in a company register, and can appoint managers to represent the company.

Partner governance is predictable. The general assembly of all partners must meet at least once a year, within six months of the year end. Partners holding at least 10% of the capital can call a meeting, decisions can be taken by circulation, and meetings can be held online.

Transfers are controlled. If a partner wants to sell to an outsider, the other partners have a right of first refusal, and the company itself can buy, within 30 days of the manager being told the agreed price, unless the articles set a longer period. That gives existing partners real protection against unwanted newcomers. What surprises many founders is how far the 2022 law brought LLCs towards investor-style terms: with the approval of partners holding at least 90% of the capital, the articles can include drag-along and tag-along rights. The articles can also send disputes among partners, or between the company and its managers, to arbitration.

One warning sign to diarise. If an LLC’s losses reach half its capital, the manager must call the general assembly within 60 days of becoming aware of it, to decide whether to continue, with measures to address the losses, or to dissolve. Founders who capitalise an LLC thinly can trip this sooner than they expect.

The simplified joint-stock company: built for growth

The simplified joint-stock company is the most significant innovation in the current Companies Law, and for venture-backed startups it is often the right answer. It combines the share-based structure of a joint-stock company with the flexibility of a private company.

  • No statutory minimum capital. The law expressly disapplies the joint-stock company minimum. The articles state the issued and paid-up capital and can authorise additional capital for future issues.
  • Governance you design. The shareholders regulate the company’s structure and procedures in the articles. It can be run by one or more presidents or managers, a board, or any other arrangement, and shareholders exercise the powers of the general assembly directly or through a designee.
  • Investor protections in the articles. With unanimous shareholder approval, the articles can restrict share transfers for up to 10 years from issue (extendable unanimously), require approval before any transfer, set conditions under which a shareholder must sell, and refer disputes to arbitration. Transfers that breach the restrictions are void.
  • Single-shareholder option. One person can form a simplified joint-stock company, with liability limited to the capital they commit.

Two practical points. The incorporation file must include a certificate of paid-up capital from a bank licensed in the Kingdom, so the bank relationship comes before incorporation rather than after. And converting an existing company into a simplified joint-stock company later requires the unanimous approval of all partners or shareholders, which is easy with two founders and much harder with ten. If you expect to raise a round, choosing this form at the start usually saves a reorganisation.

The joint-stock company: for scale and the capital market

A conventional joint-stock company can be formed by one or more persons, with capital divided into tradable shares and shareholders liable only up to the value of their shares. Its issued capital must be at least SAR 500,000, with at least a quarter paid up on incorporation, and it comes with the full apparatus of board elections, general assemblies and disclosure rules.

For most new ventures that is more structure than they need. The joint-stock company makes sense for larger businesses, those with many shareholders, and companies preparing for a listing, where capital market rules apply on top of the Companies Law.

Partnerships: rarely the right choice for new ventures

In a general partnership, two or more partners are jointly and personally liable for the firm’s debts, and each partner acquires merchant status. A limited partnership pairs at least one general partner, who carries that unlimited liability, with at least one limited partner, whose liability is capped at their capital. Limited partners have defined inspection rights, twice a year, but if they interfere in the company’s external dealings, even under a power of attorney, they can become personally liable for the resulting debts.

Partnerships still have niche uses, such as a passive investor backing an active operator. But given that an LLC offers limited liability with similar flexibility, most advisers steer new businesses away from them.

Professional companies and other special cases

Professional companies. Licensed professionals, such as engineers, lawyers and accountants, can practise through a professional company, which can take any of the five forms. A single licensed professional can use a professional joint-stock company, simplified joint-stock company or single-owner LLC. A professional company can be formed jointly with a non-Saudi professional company, and unlicensed persons can be partners or shareholders, but not general partners. For foreign-owned professional firms, MISA requires at least 25% Saudi participation where there is a Saudi partner, with both partners licensed in the same field. A non-professional partner’s share cannot exceed 30%. MISA also registers professional activities separately from non-service activities, which matters if you want to combine advice with product sales.

Non-profit companies. The law allows public non-profit companies in joint-stock form and private non-profit companies in LLC form, whose surplus must be spent on approved non-profit purposes.

Structuring with foreign ownership

Foreign founders use the same company forms, typically an LLC or a simplified joint-stock company, but three extra layers apply.

MISA comes first. MISA registers legal entities, foreign companies and individuals who hold Premium Residency, and registration must be in place before you invest. Whether you need a Saudi partner depends on the activity.

Activity conditions can override the Companies Law. MISA’s Investor Guide sets minimum capital and Saudi participation for certain activities. Trading with a Saudi partner needs SAR 26,666,667 of capital and at least 25% Saudi ownership. Fully foreign-owned trading needs SAR 30,000,000, presence in at least three regional or global markets and further localisation, training and investment commitments over the first five years. Communications activities need at least 40% Saudi participation, and supportive communications activities 30%.

An auditor is required. Micro and small companies are generally exempt from appointing an auditor, but that exemption does not apply to a foreign company. Nor does it apply to companies in a group, unless every company in the group is micro or small.

Branch or subsidiary? A foreign company can register a branch instead of forming a Saudi company, and a regional headquarters can be either. A subsidiary ring-fences liability in the Saudi entity and is generally easier to bring partners into. A branch keeps the operation within the parent. That choice also affects tax and the documents you assemble, so take advice in both jurisdictions. A foreign manufacturer that only needs to support its Saudi agents and distributors can use MISA’s scientific and technical office registration instead, but such an office is barred from contracts, commercial activity and investment in the Kingdom.

How the choice plays out

Two Saudi co-founders opening a specialty café. An LLC gives them limited liability, a simple manager structure and a right of first refusal if either wants to sell. They write their shareholder agreement into the articles, including what happens if one founder leaves, and use arbitration for disputes. Neither needs an auditor while the company stays micro or small, unless the articles require one.

A foreign SaaS founder planning a seed round. A simplified joint-stock company lets the founder issue shares to investors and an employee pool, impose vesting-style transfer restrictions and compulsory transfers on leavers, and design a lean board. The bank capital certificate is arranged before incorporation, MISA registration comes first, and as a foreign-owned company it appoints an auditor from year one.

A family converting its trading establishment into a company. The family transfers the establishment’s assets into an LLC and adopts a family charter covering who can own shares, how family members are employed and how dividends are decided. The owner remains liable for the establishment’s pre-conversion debts unless creditors agree otherwise, so the conversion is timed after key liabilities are settled.

Common mistakes

  • Choosing an establishment for a business that will carry real liabilities, then having to restructure with the old debts still attached.
  • Starting as an LLC when a funding round is already planned. Converting later needs unanimous consent.
  • Copying generic articles. The Ministry of Commerce publishes templates for each company form, which are a sensible starting point, but exits, deadlock and leaver provisions need tailoring.
  • Ignoring MISA activity conditions when sizing capital for a foreign-owned trading business.
  • Forgetting the auditor for foreign-owned and group companies, and missing the six-month window to deposit financial statements.

Once you have chosen the form, the next steps are MISA registration if any owner is foreign, then the commercial registration. The full sequence is in our guide to starting a business in Saudi Arabia.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most common company type in Saudi Arabia for foreigners?

Foreign founders typically use a limited liability company or a simplified joint-stock company, after registering with MISA. A foreign company can also register a branch, and regional headquarters can be either a company or a registered branch.

Is there a minimum capital for an LLC in Saudi Arabia?

The Companies Law sets no general minimum capital for an LLC. Partners fix the capital in the articles and confirm it is fully paid. For foreign-owned businesses, MISA sets minimums for some activities, such as SAR 30,000,000 for 100% foreign-owned trading.

Can one person own a company in Saudi Arabia?

Yes. A single person can form an LLC, a simplified joint-stock company or a joint-stock company. In a single-owner LLC or simplified joint-stock company, the owner’s decisions are recorded in writing in a company register.

What is the difference between an LLC and a simplified joint-stock company?

Both limit owners’ liability. An LLC has non-tradable interests with a right of first refusal on sales to outsiders. A simplified joint-stock company has shares, governance designed in its articles, and can impose transfer restrictions of up to 10 years, compulsory transfers and arbitration, which suits investor-backed startups.

Does my company need an auditor?

Micro and small companies are generally exempt, unless their articles require one, they are listed, they issue certain instruments, another law requires it, they are foreign companies, or they belong to a group that is not entirely micro or small. Partners holding at least 10% can also request one.

Can I convert my establishment into a company later?

Yes. The Companies Law lets owners of sole proprietorships transfer their assets into a company of any form. The owner stays liable for debts incurred before the conversion unless creditors expressly agree otherwise.
