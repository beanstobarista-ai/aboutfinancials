How to start a business in Saudi Arabia

Setting up in the Kingdom is more digital and more joined-up than its reputation suggests. Once you see the sequence, it becomes predictable: who owns the business decides where you start, what the business does decides which licences follow, and a small set of national platforms carries you from registration to your first hire. This guide walks through the whole journey the way an experienced adviser would lay it out for a founder.

Information as of 10 October 2026 · Checked against official Saudi sources · About 20 min read

At a glance

  1. Settle ownership and activity first. Saudi and GCC-owned businesses go straight to the Ministry of Commerce. Any foreign ownership means registering with the Ministry of Investment (MISA) before you invest.
  2. Pick the legal vehicle, usually an establishment, a limited liability company or a simplified joint-stock company.
  3. Issue the commercial registration (CR) through the Saudi Business Center. It opens your files with the other agencies.
  4. Secure premises, a national address and the municipal licence through Balady, plus any sector licence your activity needs.
  5. Register for zakat or income tax, and for VAT once taxable sales pass SAR 375,000 over 12 months.
  6. Open the bank account, set up Qiwa and GOSI, and handle residency for any expatriate staff.
  7. Diary the annual obligations: CR confirmation, MISA update, financial statements and tax returns.

Start with two questions: who owns it, and what will it do?

Almost every decision in a Saudi setup flows from two facts: the nationality of the people and companies that own the business, and the activity it will carry on. Get those right on day one and the rest of the process tends to fall into place. Get them wrong and you can find yourself re-filing applications weeks later.

Ownership sets your entry point. Saudi nationals, and GCC citizens and companies fully owned by them, are treated as local investors. They apply directly through the Ministry of Commerce and never deal with MISA. The moment a foreign individual or a foreign-owned company sits anywhere in the ownership, the business needs MISA investment registration before it invests. That includes a GCC company with foreign shareholders further up its own structure, which MISA no longer treats as local. Only after MISA confirms the registration does the foreign-owned business move on to commercial registration and sector licences.

Activity sets your licences. Under the Investment Law, foreign investors can in principle invest in any activity, apart from a published list of excluded activities. Activities on that list need MISA’s prior approval, which MISA refers to the relevant authority. Separately, some open activities come with their own conditions, such as a minimum Saudi shareholding or a minimum capital. And regardless of ownership, many activities need a licence from a sector regulator or the municipality before you can trade.

One group deserves a special mention: holders of Saudi Premium Residency. They can register with MISA as individuals, and they are exempt from the home-country commercial register and financial statements that MISA asks foreign companies to provide. For a foreign founder already living in the Kingdom under Premium Residency, that removes the slowest part of the paperwork.

If your shareholding is mixed, or you are unsure whether a partner makes the business “foreign”, our MISA investment registration guide works through the edge cases.

The platforms you will use, and how they connect

Founders often feel overwhelmed by the number of government portals. In practice you will spend most of your time on a handful, and they share data with each other, so information entered once usually flows through. Here is how they fit together.

Two other channels are worth knowing about. MISA runs comprehensive service centres for investors, which help with guidance and coordinate licences with other authorities, though the registration itself is still filed online. And One-Stop Services centres, in cities such as Jeddah and Dammam, support many of the government services that MISA’s Investor Guide lists for registered investors, from residency matters to Ministry of Justice requests.

Step 1: Pin down your activity

This sounds administrative, but it is the most strategic step in the whole process. The activities you register shape the licences you need, the conditions MISA applies, the Saudization expectations you will face and even how easy it is to add a business line later.

MISA registers activities against the national classification of economic activities, and every activity falls into one of two tracks: available or restricted. Available activities move through the standard review. Restricted activities need prior approval and take longer. Either way, MISA says its review takes up to 10 working days once your file is complete.

A few categories carry extra conditions that are worth checking before you commit. MISA’s Investor Guide lists, among others:

ActivityCondition in MISA’s Investor Guide
Trading with a Saudi partnerMinimum capital of SAR 26,666,667 and at least 25% Saudi ownership
Trading, 100% foreign-ownedMinimum capital of SAR 30,000,000, presence in at least three regional or global markets, plus localisation, training and five-year investment commitments
Communications activitiesAt least 40% Saudi ownership
Professional activities with a Saudi partnerAt least 25% Saudi ownership, both partners licensed in the same profession
Engineering consulting, 100% foreign-ownedPresence in at least four countries and at least 10 years of experience
Legal services, 100% foreign-ownedApproval letter from the Ministry of Justice

Notice what is not on that list: most technology, services, hospitality and manufacturing businesses. For many founders the activity check is a formality. For trading businesses it is the single biggest decision in the project, because the capital threshold for full foreign ownership is high enough to change the whole business case.

Two practical points. MISA registers professional activities separately and will not combine them with non-service activities on the same registration, so a consulting firm that also wants to sell products needs to think about structure. And while there is no cap on the number of activities you can register, MISA expects every activity you add to be practised, so resist the temptation to register everything you might do one day.

Adviser’s tip: write a one-paragraph description of what the business will actually sell, to whom and from where, before you open any portal. Then map that description to the official activity codes. Founders who start from the code list tend to over-register or pick an activity that triggers a licence they did not need.

Step 2: Choose the legal vehicle

The Companies Law offers five company forms: general partnership, limited partnership, joint-stock company, simplified joint-stock company and limited liability company. A Saudi individual can also trade as an establishment, a sole proprietorship registered in the owner’s own name, and a foreign company can operate through a branch.

In practice most new businesses choose between three options. The establishment is the quickest route for a Saudi owner-operator with modest risk. The limited liability company is the workhorse for owner-managed and foreign-owned businesses: it can have one owner or several, it ring-fences the owners’ liability to their capital, and it needs no board. The simplified joint-stock company is built for businesses that expect to bring in investors: it has no statutory minimum capital, lets shareholders design their own governance, and supports share restrictions, compulsory transfers and arbitration clauses that venture investors look for. A conventional joint-stock company, by contrast, needs issued capital of at least SAR 500,000, a quarter of it paid up on incorporation.

The decision turns on liability, how many owners you have and plan to have, whether you will raise money, how much governance you want to carry, and whether foreign ownership triggers an auditor. We compare the options side by side, with the trade-offs, in Saudi business structures explained.

Step 3: Register with MISA (foreign ownership only)

If any owner is foreign, this is your first formal filing, and you should treat it as the critical path. The application itself is online through the Invest Saudi portal and does not require a visit. What takes time is assembling the documents.

For a standard registration, MISA asks a foreign company for a copy of its home commercial register and its financial statements for the last financial year, both authenticated by the Saudi embassy, plus anything specific to the activity. Authentication is done through Saudi missions abroad for each partner, in each partner’s own country. MISA does not require the documents to be translated, though other ministries may. MISA also collects ownership details, including those who control the investor or are its ultimate beneficial owners.

Once the file is complete, MISA notifies you within 10 working days. If something is missing, it gives you a deadline of at least 15 working days to fix it, after which the application lapses. On approval MISA tells you the registration fee, which must be paid within 15 business days or the registration is void. The certificate is issued in Arabic only.

Not every foreign business uses the standard track. MISA offers entrepreneurial registration for innovative startups backed by a Saudi university or an accredited business incubator, temporary registration for foreign companies delivering a government contract, and dedicated registrations for regional headquarters, scientific and technical offices and economic liaison offices. Each has its own documents and obligations, set out in our MISA guide.

Step 4: Incorporate and issue the commercial registration

Every merchant, whether an individual or a company, must be entered in the Commercial Register, and the whole process now runs electronically through the Saudi Business Center. For a company, this is the moment the business legally comes into existence: a company acquires legal personality on registration. Until then, anyone signing contracts on its behalf carries personal exposure if the incorporation is not completed.

The application brings together your trade name, legal form, capital, the names and powers of your managers or directors, and the address of your principal place of business. Companies also file their articles of incorporation or association, which must be in Arabic, may carry a translation, and can be based on templates the Ministry of Commerce publishes for each company form. The company form must appear in the trade name. For a foreign-owned business, the filing on the Ministry of Commerce portal is made by the contact officer registered with MISA, and the articles are authenticated and the CR issued electronically.

The registrar must decide on a complete application within 10 days. Under the 2024 Commercial Register Law you can register several activities on a single CR, even unrelated ones. The new law also drops the old periodic renewal: instead, you confirm the CR’s data every year.

The CR is also the trigger for the rest of the system. The Ministry of Commerce describes the Saudi Business Center as opening the business’s files with the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development, ZATCA, GOSI, Saudi Post’s national address service and the Chamber of Commerce when the CR is issued, and ZATCA confirms that a tax identification number is generated once the business is registered with the Ministry of Commerce. The full process, including the annual confirmation and what happens if you miss it, is in our commercial registration guide.

Step 5: Premises, national address and the municipal licence

Most businesses that serve customers from a physical location need a municipal commercial licence, and this is where founders most often underestimate the timeline. The licence is issued through the Balady platform, together with a Civil Defense safety permit, and the application is filed electronically against your CR, activity and premises.

Balady asks for an exterior photo of the shop showing its signboard, a lease, ownership deed or investment contract, a copy of the building permit, a safety equipment invoice or Civil Defense safety report for activities approved instantly, and, depending on the activity, a sanitation contract. The Ministry of Municipalities and Housing gives a service time of 1 to 10 days. Activities that qualify for an instant licence are issued once the fee is paid, while others go to the municipality for review. Before you sign a lease, use Balady’s municipal requirements enquiry to check what your specific activity needs. The premises have to fit the activity, not the other way round.

Every premises also needs a national address, the Saudi Post addressing system that government services rely on. Registration is free. The address combines a four-digit building number, street, a four-digit secondary number, district, a five-digit postal code and city, and it can also be expressed as a short code of four letters and four numbers.

Once you trade, two certificates go on the wall. The Commercial Register Law requires you to display your CR data in a conspicuous place at the business premises, and a VAT-registered business must display its VAT certificate at its main place of business and branches.

Step 6: Sector licences

The CR and municipal licence get you open. They do not, on their own, authorise regulated activities. MISA is explicit that after registration a foreign investor must obtain “the necessary licences from other competent authorities”, and the same applies to Saudi-owned businesses in regulated sectors. Healthcare, education, food manufacturing, media, financial services and transport each have their own regulator with its own conditions.

The sensible order is to check the sector licence conditions before you incorporate, because they can affect your capital, premises, staffing and even your choice of activity code. For foreign investors, MISA’s comprehensive service centre is designed to coordinate these approvals with the relevant authority, which can save a lot of back and forth.

Step 7: Zakat, income tax and VAT

Saudi Arabia has two regimes for business profits, and which one applies depends on who owns the business. Saudi and GCC nationals, and the establishments they own, pay zakat. ZATCA’s small business guide sets it at 2.5% for each Hijri year, with registration after the CR is issued and before the end of the first financial year, and the return due within 120 days of the year end. Foreign owners are subject to corporate income tax instead, with the same registration window and a 120-day return deadline. In a company with both Saudi and foreign shareholders, ZATCA applies income tax to the foreign partners’ share, so mixed-ownership companies deal with both regimes and need an accountant who handles the split routinely.

Two other taxes catch founders out early. If you pay a non-resident supplier, such as an overseas software vendor or consultant, you may have to withhold tax and file the withholding form by the tenth day of the following month. And VAT becomes mandatory once your taxable sales exceed SAR 375,000 over 12 months. You can register voluntarily from SAR 187,500, and the application must be made within 30 days of the end of the month in which you cross the mandatory threshold. The standard rate has been 15% since 1 July 2020, and VAT-registered businesses must issue invoices through a compliant e-invoicing solution. Our VAT registration guide covers thresholds, timing, returns and e-invoicing in detail.

Step 8: Bank account and capital

Banking is the step founders most often leave too late. For a simplified joint-stock company it actually comes before incorporation: the Companies Law requires a certificate from a bank licensed in the Kingdom confirming the paid-up capital to be filed with the articles of association. For other forms, the Commercial Register Law allows the Ministry of Commerce to require bank account details after registration.

Each bank sets its own onboarding requirements, and those are not published in one official place. Expect to provide your CR, your articles, your national address and identification for the signatories and owners, and allow extra time if your owners or signatories are outside the Kingdom. Ask the bank for its document list in writing before you start.

Step 9: Hiring and residency

Your first hire brings three more systems into play. Qiwa, the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development’s platform, holds your establishment file and handles services such as work permits and transfers of employees’ sponsorship from other employers. MISA’s Investor Guide notes that the ministry’s services for investors require a registered Saudi authorised representative listed as a user or account manager of the establishment, so decide early who will hold that role.

GOSI registration follows. GOSI’s employer guidance says occupational hazards cover is mandatory for every worker, whatever their sex, nationality or age, while the annuities (pension) branch covers Saudi nationals. Contribution rates depend on the employee and, for Saudi employees, on when they were first registered, so take current rates from GOSI itself rather than from older articles.

For expatriate staff, residency permits, exit and re-entry visas and related services run through Absher and Muqeem. If you are bringing in a general manager, MISA’s Investor Guide lists what a new residency permit requires: the CR showing the general manager’s name, an electronic work permit from the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development, a medical report and a health insurance certificate. Localisation (Saudization) rates are set by the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development, and some MISA registration categories add their own localisation and training commitments, so build Saudi hiring into your plan from the start.

How long does it really take?

The official service times are genuinely short: up to 10 working days for a MISA decision, 10 days for the registrar to decide on a complete CR application, and 1 to 10 days for a Balady commercial licence. The Kingdom’s systems are fast once a file is complete.

What stretches timelines is everything around those steps. Embassy authentication of home-country documents for each foreign partner, finding premises that meet municipal and Civil Defense requirements, sector licences with their own review periods, and bank onboarding all sit outside the government service levels. We do not publish an overall “days to launch” figure, because there is no official one and it varies enormously by activity. Plan the critical path around your slowest dependency, which for most foreign founders is document authentication and for most retail and food businesses is the premises.

What it costs

There is no single official “cost to start a business” in Saudi Arabia, and you should be wary of articles that quote one. Each authority sets its own fees, and MISA only confirms its registration fee when it approves your application. Some steps are free: ZATCA’s VAT registration and Saudi Post’s national address registration cost nothing. Balady provides a fee calculator for municipal licences.

For budgeting, separate the one-off setup costs (government fees, embassy authentication, any translations other ministries require, professional advice and fit-out) from the recurring costs of staying compliant (annual MISA update fees for foreign-owned businesses, Chamber membership, accounting and, where required, audit). Staying in good standing: your annual calendar

Registration is the start of your relationship with these agencies, not the end. The obligations below are where young businesses most often slip, and several carry automatic consequences.

  • Annual CR confirmation. Confirm your CR data every year. If you are 90 days late, after a 14-day warning, the registration is suspended and every licence issued under it is suspended too.
  • Changes within 15 days. Update the CR within 15 days of any change to the data it holds, such as managers, capital or address.
  • MISA annual update for foreign-owned businesses. MISA notifies you 30 working days ahead, gives you up to 30 working days to file, and lets you file within the 60 working days before the deadline. Relocating your premises or changing your account manager must be notified to MISA within 10 working days.
  • Financial statements prepared under Saudi-approved accounting standards and deposited within six months of the year end. Micro and small companies are generally exempt from appointing an auditor, but foreign companies and a few other cases are not.
  • Zakat or income tax returns within 120 days of the year end, and VAT returns monthly or quarterly once registered.
  • Records kept for at least six years from the end of the VAT period they relate to.

Three founder scenarios

A Saudi café owner in Riyadh. With Saudi ownership there is no MISA step. The decisive early work is the site: before signing the lease, the owner checks Balady’s requirements for the café activity and confirms the unit has a building permit that suits it. The CR is issued as an establishment or a single-owner LLC, the national address is linked, and the Balady commercial licence and safety permit follow with the lease and photos. Zakat registration is due before the end of the first year, VAT once sales pass the threshold, and Qiwa and GOSI before the first barista starts. The owner chooses an LLC if they want liability ring-fenced or plan to bring in a partner for a second branch.

A foreign SaaS founder moving to the Kingdom. A founder with incubator backing can use MISA’s entrepreneurial registration rather than the standard track, which needs a support letter from the incubator or university that names the activity. If the founder already holds Premium Residency, the home-country register and financial statements fall away. The company is usually a simplified joint-stock company so that a seed round and an employee share pool can be added later without reorganising. Because the business sells software and services, the activity check is usually simple. The real work is banking, VAT on overseas and local sales, withholding tax on foreign cloud and contractor bills, and planning Saudi hires.

A multinational setting up a regional headquarters. Regional headquarters have their own MISA registration with demanding conditions. The RHQ must be a separate legal entity, either a company or a registered branch. It may not earn commercial revenue outside its licensed activities. It must start its mandatory activities within six months and at least three optional activities within a year, and employ at least 15 full-time staff within a year, including at least three at executive director or vice-president level. Groups that meet its eligibility criteria can also look at MISA’s Strategic Investor Program, a package of support services for national and foreign companies.

Mistakes that cost founders time and money

  • Investing before registering with MISA. Engaging in an investment activity without registration is a serious violation. The penalties can include a warning, a fine of up to SAR 300,000, doubled for repeat violations, or cancellation of the registration.
  • Signing a lease before checking municipal requirements. If the unit does not suit the activity, the licence fails and the lease remains.
  • Registering activities “just in case”. Every activity can carry licence conditions, and MISA expects registered activities to be practised.
  • Treating the CR as a one-off. The annual confirmation is easy to miss and suspension is automatic, with your licences suspended alongside it.
  • Forgetting MISA after a shareholder change. Ownership changes go to MISA first, and the partners’ resolution and CR must then be updated within 30 days, or MISA can restore the previous registration data.
  • Relying on pre-2025 guides. The Investment Law replaced MISA “licences” with registration, and the 2024 Commercial Register Law replaced periodic CR renewal with an annual confirmation. Older articles describe processes that no longer exist.

Frequently asked questions

Can a foreigner own 100% of a business in Saudi Arabia?

In many activities, yes. The Investment Law allows foreign investment in any activity not on the excluded list, and MISA confirms that some activities need a local partner while others do not. A few categories set minimum Saudi ownership or capital. For example, 100% foreign-owned trading requires SAR 30,000,000 of capital and presence in at least three markets, according to MISA’s Investor Guide.

Do I still need a MISA licence?

No. MISA licences have been replaced by investment registration under the updated Investment Law. Foreign investors must register with MISA before investing, and Saudi and GCC owners do not need MISA at all.

How long does it take to start a business in Saudi Arabia?

The official service times are short: up to 10 working days for a MISA decision, 10 days for a complete CR application and 1 to 10 days for a Balady commercial licence. The overall timeline depends mostly on document authentication, premises and any sector licences, so there is no single official figure.

Which government platforms will I need?

Typically the Invest Saudi portal (foreign owners only), the Saudi Business Center for the CR, Saudi Post’s national address, Balady for the municipal licence, ZATCA for tax, Qiwa for your establishment file and work permits, GOSI for social insurance, and Absher and Muqeem for residency matters.

Do I pay zakat or corporate income tax?

Saudi and GCC owners pay zakat and foreign owners pay corporate income tax. In a mixed company, ZATCA applies income tax to the non-Saudi partners’ share. Both registrations are due before the end of the first financial year, and both returns within 120 days of the year end.

When do I need to register for VAT?

When taxable sales exceed SAR 375,000 over 12 months. Apply within 30 days of the end of the month in which you crossed the threshold. You can register voluntarily from SAR 187,500.

Does a commercial registration expire?

The 2024 Commercial Register Law does not provide for periodic renewal. Instead, you confirm your CR data every year. If the confirmation is 90 days late, the CR is suspended, along with every licence issued under it.

Do I need a physical office?

Your CR needs a principal place of business address, and businesses serving customers from premises need a Balady commercial licence tied to a lease or deed. Whether a particular activity can run without dedicated premises depends on its licence conditions, so check Balady’s requirements for your activity.
